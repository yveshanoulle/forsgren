Legend

Every number counts back from the time in the footer: deployment frequency from the successful deployments, lead time for changes from the commits they shipped, failed deployment recovery time from the failed deployments and the successful ones after them, change fail rate from the failed deployments and the issues labelled failure, deployment rework rate from the successful deployments that came after a failed deployment or while a failure issue was open.

{{if .DoraDaysSet}}The window is {{.Window}} days (dora_days in forsgren.config.yml).{{else}}The window is {{.Window}} days (the default; dora_days is not set in forsgren.config.yml).{{end}}

A project's total row counts all its repositories; a row under it counts what its label names in the project's config. Durations: min is minutes, h hours, d days, each cut down, never rounded up.

The next column, Weekend deployments, is how many of the successful deployments that count were created on a Saturday or a Sunday, UTC, over the same {{if eq .Window 180}}180{{else}}{{.Window}} or 180{{end}} days; it is 0 when there are none. The numbers view shows the last {{.Window}} days.

Lead time for changes

The DORA band, then the median time from a commit to the successful deployment that shipped it, and in brackets the commits deployed in the last {{.Window}} days. A month is 30 days:

  • More than six months: 180 days and more, score {{index .LeadTimeScores 5}}

Failed deployment recovery time

The DORA band, on the edges of lead time for changes, as the DORA Quick Check gives both the same answers; then the median time from the first failed deployment of a run to the next successful deployment of the same stream, and in brackets the recoveries of the last {{.Window}} days; then the recoveries not completed yet, each a run of failed deployments with no success after it, counted, not timed. With none completed, the band and the median are —.

  • Recovered in less than one hour: below 1 hour, score {{index .RecoveryScores 0}}
  • Recovered in less than one day: 1 hour to below 24 hours, score {{index .RecoveryScores 1}}
  • Recovered in one day to one week: 24 hours to below 7 days, score {{index .RecoveryScores 2}}
  • Recovered in one week to one month: 7 days to below 30 days, score {{index .RecoveryScores 3}}
  • Recovered in one to six months: 30 days to below 180 days, score {{index .RecoveryScores 4}}
  • Recovered in more than six months: 180 days and more, score {{index .RecoveryScores 5}}

Change fail rate

The DORA band, the nearest label of the DORA Quick Check's scale; then the failed changes out of the final deployments of the last {{.Window}} days, in whole percent, rounded down, and in brackets both counts. A failed change is a failed deployment or an issue labelled failure opened in the window, per repository the larger of the two counts, so an issue about a failed deployment is not counted twice. A failure issue names no service, so a service's row counts its failed deployments only.

  • 0%: below 10%, score {{index .ChangeFailScores 0}}
  • 20%: 10% to below 30%, score {{index .ChangeFailScores 1}}
  • 40%: 30% to below 50%, score {{index .ChangeFailScores 2}}
  • 60%: 50% to below 70%, score {{index .ChangeFailScores 3}}
  • 80%: 70% to below 90%, score {{index .ChangeFailScores 4}}
  • 100%: 90% and more, score {{index .ChangeFailScores 5}}

Deployment rework rate

The DORA band, on the six points and edges of change fail rate, as the DORA Quick Check scales both the same; then the rework deployments out of the successful deployments of the last {{.Window}} days, in whole percent, rounded down, and in brackets both counts. A rework deployment is a successful deployment that is the first success of its stream after a failed deployment, or that happened while an issue labelled failure of its repository was open; it is counted once. A failure issue names no service, so a service's row counts the first kind only. DORA's question is "Approximately what percentage of deployments in the last 6 months were not planned but were performed to address a user-facing bug in the application?", but forsgren measures the last {{.Window}} days, like its other four metrics.

  • Rework of 0%: below 10% of the deployments, score {{index .ReworkScores 0}}
  • Rework of 20%: 10% to below 30% of the deployments, score {{index .ReworkScores 1}}
  • Rework of 40%: 30% to below 50% of the deployments, score {{index .ReworkScores 2}}
  • Rework of 60%: 50% to below 70% of the deployments, score {{index .ReworkScores 3}}
  • Rework of 80%: 70% to below 90% of the deployments, score {{index .ReworkScores 4}}
  • Rework of 100%: 90% and more of the deployments, score {{index .ReworkScores 5}}

Scores

The scoring view shows each metric's score, 0 to 10, and Overall Performance, the mean of the scored metrics, to one decimal. The Numbers and scoring page, when it is shown, has the number and the score of each metric in one cell. Scores follow the DORA Quick Check (dora.dev), © Google LLC, CC BY 4.0.

Each metric's levels above show the score they give; change fail rate and deployment rework rate are scored per whole percent, so their level shows the range of scores its percentages give.

Issues pages

Days are in UTC, weeks run Monday to Sunday, months and years follow the calendar, and a week that spans two months is in both.

Open is the issues open at the end of the period. Total is the issues created up to the end of the period, open or closed; a reopened issue is not created again. Open / Total is Open out of Total, in whole percent, rounded down, and a dash when Total is 0.

New, Completed, Not planned, Duplicate and Other count what happened in the row's period (UTC): New is the issues created; Completed, Not planned and Duplicate are the issues closed with that reason; Other is the issues closed with no reason, or with a reason forsgren does not know, so every close is counted in exactly one of the four. A reopen is counted in none of them; it only puts the issue back into Open.

Lead time is the median time from an issue's creation to its Completed close, over the issues completed in the row's period; Not planned, Duplicate and Other closes are not counted. A reopened issue is measured from its creation to the close in that period. Bugs lead time is the same over the issues that carry the GitHub label bug, and shows — (incomplete) on the same rule as Open bugs. A dash means no issue was completed in the period.

Open bugs is Open counted over the issues that carry the GitHub label bug, as the label is at the latest collection, so adding or removing the label moves the issue's whole history.

When the bug list of a repository is too long to read in full (the read stops at 10,000 issues), Open bugs shows — (incomplete) instead of a number in every row, because a partial count would look complete. The number returns by itself after a complete read.

An arrow after Open and after Open bugs shows which way the count moved from the end of the period before: red when it rose, green when it fell, and grey when it did not change (blue after Open bugs, where a count of 0 open bugs is green too). Red and green mean rose and fell, not worse and better. A day is compared with the day before, a week with the week before, a month with the month before and a year with the year before; the oldest row of each kind is compared with the count just before its period, even when that period is outside the rows shown.

Two limits: a reopen is dated when the collection sees it, not when it happened, so it can fall in a later period than the real one; and when a repository has more issues than the one full read takes (it stops at 100 pages of 100 issues), its older issues are missing and its historical Total stays incomplete. The — (incomplete) note covers Open bugs and Bugs lead time only.

Each optional page has a key in forsgren.config.yml: issues_page, days_page, numbers_scoring_page, history_page and tbd_page. A key takes true (the page is generated and in the menu), unlisted (generated, with no menu entry, and search engines are asked to skip it) or false (the page is not generated). An unlisted page can still be opened by its address. By default the Issues page is listed (issues_page: true), the Daily page is not generated (days_page: false), and the History page, the Numbers and scoring page and the TBD page are unlisted (history_page: unlisted, numbers_scoring_page: unlisted, tbd_page: unlisted). To put the Daily page in the menu, set days_page: true and run forsgren again to regenerate the report. The Numbers and scoring page puts each metric's number and its score in one cell, written number · score, with a dash where there is no data.

History page{{beta}}

The History page shows the five metrics and Overall Performance for each period, the oldest on the left. The periods are the calendar half-years (January to June, July to December, UTC) that history_days reaches back, and Last 6 months, the newest column, which runs up to the render time from 180 days before it, so it overlaps the current half-year. A period is a column only when some row has data for it. While only the last 6 months have data, a line under the heading gives the first deployment and the day the first half-year appears. dora_days does not change this page.

Every period is banded as 180 days, DORA's standard, with the edges of 180 days at a working day of {{.WorkingDay}} hours: weekly to monthly from {{.HistoryEdges.WeeklyToMonthly}}, daily to weekly from {{.HistoryEdges.DailyToWeekly}}, hourly to daily from {{.HistoryEdges.HourlyToDaily}} and on demand from {{.HistoryOnDemandFrom}}. A half-year has 181 to 184 days and the count is the period's own count, not rescaled to 180, so it can read higher than the same pace over exactly 180 days, by at most 3%.

A cell is the number and its score, written number · score, as on the Numbers and scoring page. Above each project's rows a chart draws the score of each measure per period, one line each, 0 to 10, 10 at the top, the same scale in every chart; a legend under it names each line by its colour and its dash pattern. A period without data is a gap in the line, never a zero, and a period alone between gaps is a dot. The charts are shown from two periods on.

A dash means no data: nothing was deployed yet in that period, or the collection has not read the history back that far yet. To read further back, raise history_days, up to 1825 (5 years); the page fills in over the next daily runs. The lead time of older periods can be a dash, because the commits of older deployments may not be stored. The change fail rate of an old period can count a failure issue that lost its label long ago, because the catch-up of withdrawn failure issues covers only the dora_days window.

The History page is unlisted by default (history_page: unlisted): it is generated and can be opened at history.html, but it has no menu entry and search engines are asked to skip it. Set history_page: true to put it in the menu, or history_page: false to not generate it.

{{.TbdTitle}}

The TBD page (trunk-based development) shows the Mainline Interval of each project: the mean and the sample standard deviation of the time between commits landing on the main branch, by their committer date, then the number of gaps in brackets, written mean ± deviation (gaps), for example 2 h ± 1 h (3). A gap is the time between two commits that are next to each other by committer date. It counts the commits that deployments of the last {{.Window}} days shipped, counted by the date of the deployment, each commit once, even when two deployments shipped it, so it is an interval between deployed commits, not every commit of the mainline, because forsgren stores the commits that deployments shipped.

Of the commits a deployment shipped, only those of its first-parent chain count: from the deployed commit back through the first parent of each commit, the commits that landed on the main branch one after another. The commits a merge brought in from a branch are left out; the merge commit counts, at the time it landed. The committer date is when a commit landed where it is now, after a rebase or a merge; the author date is when the change was first written.

Commits stored before forsgren kept their parents and committer date have no known chain. For a deployment with those, every commit counts at its author date, the old approximation, and so does a commit of the chain that GitHub gave no committer date for. The cell then ends with how many commits are timed this way, written 2 h ± 1 h (3) · median 2 h · p90 - · 3 commits timed by author date, or 1 commit timed by author date. Those commits are not looked up again, so the note stays until their deployments leave the window. When more than half of the commits are timed this way, the interval is mostly an estimate, and the note becomes Mostly estimated: 7 of 13 commits timed by author date.

A dash means fewer than two commits, so there is no gap. With one gap the mean is shown, and the deviation is a dash, because a sample deviation needs two gaps.

Next to them the cell gives the median and the 90th percentile (p90) of the gaps, written median 5 min · p90 1 h. The median is the middle gap, or the mean of the two middle gaps when there is an even number of them. p90 is the nearest-rank percentile: the gap at position 0.9 × the number of gaps, rounded up, of the gaps sorted from the shortest, so nine tenths of the gaps are no longer than it. The median needs two gaps and p90 needs ten gaps. With fewer than ten gaps p90 would only be the longest gap. Each is a dash until then. A mean far above the median, and a deviation far above the mean, point at a few long gaps among short ones.

Next to the Mainline Interval, Developers is the number of people who made the commits deployed in the window, those a merge brought in included: the distinct GitHub accounts of their authors, so a person who commits from several email addresses counts once. A bot account, whose GitHub login ends in [bot], is not a developer, and its commits are left out. A commit whose author GitHub links to no account is not guessed to be anyone's: the number leaves it out and the cell says how many, written 3 · 2 commits without a known author are not counted. GitHub's placeholder author invalid-email-address is such an author too, and not a developer. Commits in the legacy format have no known author and are not counted; the legacy format says how a record was stored, not why the author is missing. When most of the deployed commits are of that kind, the cell says so, written 3 developers · 1099 commits without a known author excluded (legacy data). A project with no counted commit has 0 developers. The page shows the number only, never who the developers are. The interval is not divided by it: a team of ten makes more commits than one person with the same habits, so read the interval next to the number.

Mainline Lead Time covers merged human pull requests only, not every change that reaches the main branch. Work pushed straight to the main branch is not in it, so a pull request stands in for the life of a branch. In a workflow that mixes both, the figure covers only the changes that came through pull requests. It shows how old a change is when it reaches the main branch: from the first commit of a pull request to its merge.

Each pull request merged in the last {{.Window}} days, counted by its merge time, gives one lead time: from its first commit, the earliest author date of its commits, to the merge. A first commit dated after the merge counts as 0. The cell is written mean ± deviation (pull requests), for example 3 h ± 1 h (4), the deviation the sample standard deviation (n-1), then 50% (2 of 4) pull requests merged within 24 hours of their first commit. The share is rounded down, and a lead time of at most 24 hours counts as within, the same boundary everywhere. There is no pass or fail verdict. A dash after the mean means fewer than two pull requests, so there is no deviation.

The two intervals are dated differently. The Mainline Interval uses the committer date: how often changes reach the main branch. Mainline Lead Time uses the author date of the first commit up to the merge time: how old changes are when they reach it.

When GitHub lists only part of a pull request's commits, its first commit time may be too late. That lead time is a lower bound: the count reads (4, 1 cut) and the cell adds the mean is a lower bound. A cut pull request can make the mean too low, and it can make the share merged within 24 hours too high. When the commit list gave no usable date, the created time of the pull request stands in for the first commit time, and the cell says how many, written 1 pull request timed by its created time, or 2 pull requests timed by their created time.

Only a GitHub account of the type User is a person. A pull request opened by a bot is not counted, and the cell says how many, written 3 bot pull requests not counted. A pull request whose author is not known to be a user is not counted either, written 2 pull requests of unknown author not counted: a pull request in the legacy format has no known author type, and neither has one by any other account type. The placeholder author invalid-email-address, which GitHub gives an author email it links to no account, is an unknown author, not a developer.

A project with no human pull request merged in the window says that no human pull request was merged in this period and that changes may go straight to the main branch. That is not proof of direct commits: it only says no human pull request was merged.

Pull requests not readable means the token forsgren collects with was refused the pull request read for a repository of the project (a 403, recorded in data/pulls_denied.csv). The pull requests of that repository are unknown, so the project shows no lead time, not an empty one. Granting the pull requests permission and a later collection removes the note. No pull requests have been collected yet means the collection has not read any pull request yet.

The two metrics are read together. The Mainline Interval shows how often the deployed commits landed on the main branch, by committer date, in the window. Mainline Lead Time shows how long the changes of pull requests take from their first commit to reaching the main branch. A short interval with a short lead time means frequent, fresh integration. A short interval with a long lead time points at long-lived branches. A long interval with a short lead time means few changes, each integrated quickly. A long interval with a long lead time means rare and old changes.

The TBD page is unlisted by default (tbd_page: unlisted): it is generated and can be opened at tbd.html, but it has no menu entry and search engines are asked to skip it. Set tbd_page: true to put it in the menu, or tbd_page: false to not generate it.

Settings page

The Default column of the settings page is the value used when forsgren.config.yml does not set the key. A current value followed by (not set) means the file does not set the key, so the default applies; a current value without it is set in the file, even to the default's value.

auto_update_level is the largest step of a forsgren update that merges itself; a larger one is left for you. By what a release asks of an installation, a patch release is fixes only, with nothing new for the installation to do or see; a minor release is new behaviour, or anything that asks the installation for something (a permission, a secret, a file, a config key); a major release is one where an installation that does nothing would stop working.

  • patch: merges patch updates only
  • minor: merges minor and patch updates
  • major: merges major, minor and patch updates. It does not mean a major release is safe: nothing checks a release against this installation's own data.

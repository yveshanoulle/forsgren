Legend

Every number counts back from the time in the footer: deployment frequency from the successful deployments, lead time for changes from the commits they shipped, failed deployment recovery time from the failed deployments and the successful ones after them and from the issues labelled failure, change fail rate from the failed deployments and the issues labelled failure, deployment rework rate from the successful deployments that came after a failed deployment or while a failure issue was open.

Change fail rate

The DORA band, the nearest label of the DORA Quick Check's scale; then the failed changes out of the final deployments of the last {{.Window}} days, in whole percent, rounded down, and in brackets both counts. A failed change is a failed deployment or an issue labelled failure opened in the window, per repository the larger of the two counts, so an issue about a failed deployment is not counted twice. A failure issue counts in the project's total row. With a line service: and a label in its failure record, the label of a service or deployment rule of its repository as this page shows it, in any case, it counts in that row too. Without the line, or with a label the repository does not have or two rows share, it counts in the total only, and the run's output names it. That is why a total can be higher than every row under it.

  • 0%: below 10%, score {{index .ChangeFailScores 0}}
  • 20%: 10% to below 30%, score {{index .ChangeFailScores 1}}
  • 40%: 30% to below 50%, score {{index .ChangeFailScores 2}}
  • 60%: 50% to below 70%, score {{index .ChangeFailScores 3}}
  • 80%: 70% to below 90%, score {{index .ChangeFailScores 4}}
  • 100%: 90% and more, score {{index .ChangeFailScores 5}}

Deployment rework rate

The DORA band, on the six points and edges of change fail rate, as the DORA Quick Check scales both the same; then the rework deployments out of the successful deployments of the last {{.Window}} days, in whole percent, rounded down, and in brackets both counts. A rework deployment is a successful deployment that is the first success of its stream after a failed deployment, or that happened while an issue labelled failure of its repository was open; it is counted once. A failure issue of the repository counts in the project's total row, and in the row of the service or deployment rule its service: line names, by the rule of change fail rate; the deployments made while it was open are rework there. So the total rate can be higher than the rate of every row under it. DORA's question is "Approximately what percentage of deployments in the last 6 months were not planned but were performed to address a user-facing bug in the application?", but forsgren measures the last {{.Window}} days, like its other four metrics.

Scores

The scoring view shows each metric's score, 0 to 10, and Overall Performance, the mean of the scored metrics, to one decimal. The Numbers and scoring page, when it is shown, has the number and the score of each metric in one cell. Scores follow the DORA Quick Check (dora.dev), © Google LLC, CC BY 4.0.

Each metric's levels above show the score they give; change fail rate and deployment rework rate are scored per whole percent, so their level shows the range of scores its percentages give.

Issues pages

Days are in UTC, weeks run Monday to Sunday, months and years follow the calendar, and a week that spans two months is in both.

Open is the issues open at the end of the period. Total is the issues created up to the end of the period, open or closed; a reopened issue is not created again. Open / Total is Open out of Total, in whole percent, rounded down, and a dash when Total is 0.

New, Completed, Not planned, Duplicate and Other count what happened in the row's period (UTC): New is the issues created; Completed, Not planned and Duplicate are the issues closed with that reason; Other is the issues closed with no reason, or with a reason forsgren does not know, so every close is counted in exactly one of the four. A reopen is counted in none of them; it only puts the issue back into Open.

Lead time is the median time from an issue's creation to its Completed close, over the issues completed in the row's period; Not planned, Duplicate and Other closes are not counted. A reopened issue is measured from its creation to the close in that period. Bugs lead time is the same over the issues that carry the GitHub label bug, and shows — (incomplete) on the same rule as Open bugs. A dash means no issue was completed in the period.

Open bugs is Open counted over the issues that carry the GitHub label bug, as the label is at the latest collection, so adding or removing the label moves the issue's whole history.

When the bug list of a repository is too long to read in full (the read stops at 10,000 issues), Open bugs shows — (incomplete) instead of a number in every row, because a partial count would look complete. The number returns by itself after a complete read.

An arrow after Open and after Open bugs shows which way the count moved from the end of the period before: red when it rose, green when it fell, and grey when it did not change (blue after Open bugs, where a count of 0 open bugs is green too). Red and green mean rose and fell, not worse and better. A day is compared with the day before, a week with the week before, a month with the month before and a year with the year before; the oldest row of each kind is compared with the count just before its period, even when that period is outside the rows shown.

Two limits: a reopen is dated when the collection sees it, not when it happened, so it can fall in a later period than the real one; and when a repository has more issues than the one full read takes (it stops at 100 pages of 100 issues), its older issues are missing and its historical Total stays incomplete. The — (incomplete) note covers Open bugs and Bugs lead time only.

Each optional page has a key in forsgren.config.yml: issues_page, days_page, numbers_scoring_page, history_page and tbd_page. A key takes true (the page is generated and in the menu), unlisted (generated, with no menu entry, and search engines are asked to skip it) or false (the page is not generated). An unlisted page can still be opened by its address. By default the Issues page and the TBD page are listed (issues_page: true, tbd_page: true), the Daily page is not generated (days_page: false), and the History page and the Numbers and scoring page are unlisted (history_page: unlisted, numbers_scoring_page: unlisted). To put the Daily page in the menu, set days_page: true and run forsgren again to regenerate the report. The Numbers and scoring page puts each metric's number and its score in one cell, written number · score, with a dash where there is no data.

History page{{beta}}

The History page shows the five metrics and Overall Performance for each period, the oldest on the left. The periods are the calendar half-years (January to June, July to December, UTC) that history_days reaches back, and Last 6 months, the newest column, which runs up to the render time from 180 days before it, so it overlaps the current half-year. A period is a column only when some row has data for it. While only the last 6 months have data, a line under the heading gives the first deployment and the day the first half-year appears. dora_days does not change this page.

Every period is banded as 180 days, DORA's standard, with the edges of 180 days at a working day of {{.WorkingDay}} hours: weekly to monthly from {{count .HistoryEdges.WeeklyToMonthly}}, daily to weekly from {{count .HistoryEdges.DailyToWeekly}}, hourly to daily from {{count .HistoryEdges.HourlyToDaily}} and on demand from {{count .HistoryOnDemandFrom}}. A half-year has 181 to 184 days and the count is the period's own count, not rescaled to 180, so it can read higher than the same pace over exactly 180 days, by at most 3%.

A cell is the number and its score, written number · score, as on the Numbers and scoring page. Above each project's rows a chart draws the score of each measure per period, one line each, 0 to 10, 10 at the top, the same scale in every chart; a legend under it names each line by its colour and its dash pattern. A period without data is a gap in the line, never a zero, and a period alone between gaps is a dot. The charts are shown from two periods on.

A dash means no data: nothing was deployed yet in that period, or the collection has not read the history back that far yet. To read further back, raise history_days, up to 1825 (5 years); the page fills in over the next daily runs. The lead time of older periods can be a dash, because the commits of older deployments may not be stored. The change fail rate of an old period can count a failure issue that lost its label long ago, because the catch-up of withdrawn failure issues covers only the dora_days window.

The History page is unlisted by default (history_page: unlisted): it is generated and can be opened at history.html, but it has no menu entry and search engines are asked to skip it. Set history_page: true to put it in the menu, or history_page: false to not generate it.

{{.TbdTitle}}

Trunk-based development means integrating small changes into the main branch often, rather than keeping changes on separate branches for a long time. These measures show how often changes are integrated, how long they take and how large they are. The TBD page is one table with a row for each project, and under it a row for each label or service. All figures cover the last {{.Window}} days, the DORA window.

How do I read the numbers?

By default, the table shows the median of each measure, the middle value. Forsgren can show the mean, the median and p90 of each measure instead; the settings page names the key. The mean is the average. p90 is the value that nine tenths of the values are no larger than. When all three figures are shown, the columns of time to main, time to deploy, commits per push and commits per deployment carry their measure for a screen reader, such as the mean of time to main.

A column that the settings turn off is not shown, and neither are the notes of its cells.

A dash means there is not enough data to calculate the figure. It does not mean zero.

Some cells carry a note marker, a number in brackets such as (3). The Notes below the table say what each number means. They explain estimates, a lower bound and a stand-in time. Missing data is shown as a dash, a no-data row or a not-readable cell, not as a note.

What are the rows under a project?

The first row of a project is its total, over all of its repositories and deployments. A project has a row under its total for each label and each service that the configuration gives it. The rows under the total are sorted by label, ignoring case. The header of such a row is the label. The Notes list gives the name of such a row as Project: label, for example Acme Shop: Website.

A label row is one repository, with every measure of that repository. A service row is one deployment task of a repository. It has its own Time to deploy per commit, Deployments, Commits per deployment and Developers, counted over the deployments of that task and the commits they shipped. A repository whose configuration lists several deployment rules has a service row for each rule, counted over the deployments of that rule. The rows under a total are shown unless the settings turn them off.

Pushes and pull requests belong to a repository, and the Mainline Interval to the project, not to a task. So in their place a service row says Per repository, once across each run of those columns.

A service can name its folders with paths in the configuration. A service with paths counts only the commits that change one of its paths, in Time to deploy per commit and Commits per deployment. A path ending in / is a folder and matches everything below it. A path without a trailing / is a file and matches that file exactly. A folder shared by several services, such as shared/, counts only for the services that list it. Files in the root of the repository, such as Scripts/ or docs, count for no service unless a service lists them. A commit that changes the paths of several services counts once for each of them. A commit that changes none counts for no service. The total row and the label rows count every commit of the repository, the commits no service counts included.

A service without paths cannot tell which commits belong to it, so it says Per repository for Time to deploy per commit and Commits per deployment: they are the repository's. The Deployments of the service stay its own.

Mainline Interval (How often do changes reach main?)

The Mainline Interval is the time between commits reaching the main branch. A shorter interval means changes are integrated more often. A longer interval means more time passes between integrations.

It measures the commits on the main line: the commits that landed on the main branch one after another. A commit that a merge brought in from a branch is not on the main line, but the merge commit is. A merge commit and a squash each add one commit to the main line. A rebase merge adds each rebased commit as its own step. Each commit is placed at the time it landed, its committer date.

Only commits that deployments of the last {{.Window}} days shipped are measured, each once. So it is an interval between deployed commits, not between every commit of the main branch. Fewer than two commits make no interval, so the cell is a dash.

When forsgren does not know the committer date of a commit, it places the commit at its author date. Note (2) says how many commits that is. When it is more than half of them, the interval is mostly an estimate, and note (1) says so.

Time to main (How long does a change take to reach main?)

Time to main per commit is how old one change is when it reaches the main branch. For each commit it runs from the author date of the commit to the push that brought it to main. Time to main is the stretch of a change's lead time from its commit to the main branch.

The pushes come from GitHub's activity list of the default branch of each repository. Push data starts when forsgren first collects it, so older pushes are not looked up afterwards.

Time to main clock (When does it start and stop?)

The clock starts at the author date of the commit: when the commit was written. It stops at the push to main that brought the commit, at the time GitHub records for that push.

A commit counts once, at its first push. A commit written after its push counts as 0.

Every lead time on this page starts at the author date of a commit, so work before the first commit is never visible. When a tool commits and pushes in one go, the commit and the push are seconds apart, except for a commit whose build fails: it waits locally until the fix on top of it is pushed.

Time to main counts the commits of the main line, merge commits included. Mainline Lead Time uses the same clock over every commit that reached main: the commits a merge brought in from a branch count, merge commits are left out, and a squash commit is replaced by the original commits of its pull request.

Mainline Interval and Time to main (How do they differ?)

The Mainline Interval is about how often changes land on main. It is the time between one commit and the next on the main line. Time to main per commit is about how old one change is when it lands. It is the time from the author date of the commit to the push that brought it.

One is a rhythm and the other is an age. A team can have a short interval and a long time to main: changes often land, but each was written long before.

Mainline Lead Time, described below, is a third number: an age like Time to main, but over every commit that reached main.

The Mainline Interval counts deployed commits, so a repository without deployments shows a dash. Time to main and Mainline Lead Time count what was pushed to main, and PR Lead Time counts merged pull requests.

Time to deploy per commit (How long does a change take to be deployed?)

Time to deploy per commit is how old one change is when it is first deployed. For each commit it runs from the author date of the commit to the first deployment that contains it. A commit written after its deployment counts as 0.

Lead time for changes (the DORA pages) and Time to deploy per commit both time a commit from its author date to a deployment, over the same last {{.Window}} days, and a negative time counts as 0. Lead time for changes counts every commit a deployment brought, also the commits a merge brought in from a branch, once for each deployment that shipped it, and shows the median with its DORA Quick Check level. Time to deploy per commit counts only the commits of the main line, a merge as its merge commit, each once, at its first deployment, and shows the median, or the mean, the median and the p90 when all three are shown. With several services the same commit is deployed several times, and Lead time for changes, which counts the later deployments too, comes out longer.

PR Lead Time (How long does a pull request take to merge?)

PR Lead Time is the time from the first commit of a pull request to its merge. The first commit is the earliest author date of its commits. It counts the pull requests merged in the last {{.Window}} days, by their merge time. A first commit dated after the merge counts as 0.

It includes only merged pull requests opened by people. Pull requests opened by bots are in the Bot PR Lead Time. Changes pushed directly to main are in neither. In a workflow that mixes both, this column covers only the changes that came through pull requests. Directly pushed changes are in the push columns, and the Direct share shows how many there are.

When GitHub lists only part of the commits of a pull request, its first commit time may be too late, so the lead time is a lower bound (note 3). When no commit date can be read, the created time of the pull request stands in for the first commit time (note 4).

A project with no merged pull request from a person shows a dash. That is not proof of direct commits: it only says no such pull request was merged.

Bot PR Lead Time (How long does a pull request of a bot take to merge?)

Bot PR Lead Time is the same measure as PR Lead Time, over the merged pull requests that a bot opened instead of a person. It has its own column right after PR Lead Time. Pull requests whose author is not known are in neither column.

It follows the same rules as PR Lead Time: the pull requests merged in the last {{.Window}} days, a first commit dated after the merge counts as 0, a cut list of commits makes it a lower bound (note 3) and a created time that stands in for the first commit time is noted (note 4). A project whose pull requests are all opened by bots, such as dependency updates, shows a dash under PR Lead Time and its figures under Bot PR Lead Time.

Mainline Lead Time (Why are there three lead times?)

The Mainline Throughput Indicator, named in Steve Smith's book Measuring Continuous Delivery, has one lead time, the Mainline Lead Time. Measured on pull requests alone, it leaves out teams that push straight to main, and pushing straight to main is very much what trunk-based development is about. So this page shows the Mainline Lead Time in full, and two views of it:

  • Mainline Lead Time, the full measure: every commit that reached main in the last {{.Window}} days, from its author date to the earliest push that brought it to main. It does not matter how the commit got there: pushed directly, brought by a merge commit, rebased or squashed.
  • PR Lead Time, for work that goes through a pull request: from the first commit of the pull request to its merge. It is a view of the merged pull requests, and it counts people only.
  • Time to main, for the commits on the main line that a push brought to main, direct pushes included: from the author date of the commit to the push that brought it. The commits a merge brought in from a branch are not counted there; the merge commit is.

A team that works with pull requests reads PR Lead Time first. A team that pushes to main reads Time to main first. A team that works both ways has both. Mainline Lead Time is the one number for all of it.

Mainline Lead Time and Time to main count different commits. The full measure leaves the merge commits themselves out: a merge commit is written at the moment of the merge, so its time would be about 0 and pull the figure down. The commits that the merge brought in from the branch stay, each from its own author date. Time to main keeps the merge commit and leaves the branch commits out. The conflict resolution inside a merge commit is not measured.

A squash merge replaces the commits of its pull request by one new commit, written at the moment of the merge. So Mainline Lead Time takes the commits of the pull request in place of the squash commit, each from its own author date, never both. A squash commit is detected when it has one parent, is the merge commit of its pull request and its push brought one commit. A squash inside a push of several commits is not detected, and counts as an ordinary commit.

The original commits of a squash count at the push of the squash commit. The merge time of the pull request never stands in for it: without that push, the originals are not counted at all.

Bots count in Mainline Lead Time, as in Time to main. PR Lead Time counts people only, and Bot PR Lead Time counts the pull requests that bots opened. The Bots share shows how many commits come from bots.

Pushes whose commits are not known, such as a force push or a push with too many commits to read, are left out: their data is incomplete, so their commits are not counted, not even as 0 (note 6).

While the last {{.Window}} days still hold data from before this measure existed, the merge and squash branch commits of that time are missing, so the figure may read short (note 7). Only merge pushes and merged pull requests from before the change set it; a direct push lost nothing and does not. The note goes away by itself once the whole window lies after the change, and also once forsgren has filled in the history of the repository (the run after this release does it once).

Its mean, median and p90 follow the same rules as the other spreads: a dash below two commits for the median and below ten for the p90, and a negative time counts as 0.

Volume (How much work is integrated at once?)

Volume shows four numbers, in the order of its columns: Commits, Pushes, Deployments and Deployed commits. Commits is the number of commits the pushes of the window brought to the main branch, each counted once. It is not every commit of the repository. Only the main line of a push counts, so a merge counts as its merge commit. Pushes is the number of pushes. Deployments is the number of successful deployments. A deployment is a GitHub release, a deployment to an environment or a run of a workflow, as the project is measured. Deployed commits is the number of distinct commits that the deployments of the window delivered. A commit counts once, at its first deployment in the window, however many deployments or services carry it. Commits are pushed commits and Deployed commits are deployed commits: a commit can be pushed in one window and deployed in another, and a service with paths counts only the commits that touch them.

Commits per push is how many commits reach main in each push. A push whose commits could not be counted, such as a force push, is left out.

Commits per deployment is how many commits on the main line a deployment brought. The commits a merge brought in from a branch count as the one merge commit. A deployment without a known main line is left out.

The mean, median and p90 of both are shown with two significant digits, rounded to the nearest, unlike the shares. Under 10 they have one decimal, always shown, such as {{decimal "1.0"}} or {{decimal "2.5"}}. From 10 they are whole numbers, such as 10 or 55.

Smaller batches are generally easier to review, test and troubleshoot.

Shares of commits (How does work reach main?)

Shares of commits shows how the commits of Volume reached main. It has four columns. All four are shares of the same Commits number, shown with two significant digits, rounded down. From 10% a share is a whole percent, such as 19% or 100%. From 1% to under 10% it has one decimal, always shown, such as {{decimal "1.3"}}% or {{decimal "3.0"}}%. Under 1% it has two decimals, such as {{decimal "0.63"}}%, and under {{decimal "0.01"}}% it reads < {{decimal "0.01"}}%. None at all is 0%.

  • Direct: commits that did not come through a pull request, such as a commit pushed directly to main or a branch merged locally and then pushed.
  • PR: commits that came through a pull request. A commit counts as PR when it is the merge commit of a merged pull request, or when a pull request merge brought it to main.
  • Unknown: commits whose author GitHub links to no account, or the placeholder author invalid-email-address.
  • Bots: commits made by automated accounts, whose login ends in [bot].

Direct and PR add up to 100%, give or take the rounding. Unknown and Bots overlap Direct and PR; they are not additional categories. A dash means no commit.

Developers (How many developers contribute?)

Developers is the number of distinct people who authored the commits deployed in the last {{.Window}} days, including the commits a merge brought in from a branch. A person who commits from several email addresses counts once. Several GitHub accounts can be configured to count as one person.

Bots and commits whose author GitHub links to no account are left out. A project with no deployed commit shows a dash. The page shows the number only, never who the developers are.

Read this number next to the others: a larger team may naturally integrate more commits.

What should I look for?

  • Short Mainline Interval and short PR Lead Time: changes are integrated often and quickly.
  • Short Mainline Interval and long PR Lead Time: changes reach main often, but some spend a long time on branches.
  • Long Mainline Interval and short PR Lead Time: changes are integrated quickly when they happen, but integration is rare.
  • Long Mainline Interval and long PR Lead Time: changes are integrated rarely and spend a long time waiting.

No single number proves that a team practises trunk-based development. Look at the measures together and watch how they change over time.

What if data is missing or incomplete?

A dash means a figure could not be calculated. A project with no deployed commit, no final deployment (a failed one counts), no push and no merged pull request in the last {{.Window}} days shows one sentence across its row: currently no data for this project in the last {{.Window}} days. It has no note markers. A project with data in any one of these keeps its figures, and a single column without data keeps its dash. A label row follows the rule for a project.

A service row with no final deployment (a failed one counts) and no deployed commit in the last {{.Window}} days shows currently no data for this service in the last {{.Window}} days. Pushes and pull requests do not count for a service.

Some figures are estimates when stored information is incomplete. The notes name these cases.

Push activity not readable means the token forsgren collects with was refused the read of the pushes of a repository of the project. Pull requests not readable means the token forsgren collects with was refused the pull request read. The affected cells say so. Deployment numbers are never shown in their place. Granting the permission and a later collection removes the message.

Pushes not collected yet means no push has been collected yet. No pull requests have been collected yet means the collection has not read any pull request yet.

Where do the names come from?

Mainline Interval and Mainline Lead Time are the two metrics of the Mainline Throughput Indicator. This page shows PR Lead Time for the work that goes through pull requests, and Time to main for every change, direct pushes included. The full Mainline Lead Time covers every commit that reached main.

Time to main is forsgren's own term. PR Lead Time as the only Mainline Lead Time did not fit teams that push straight to main, which is trunk-based development, so forsgren added a second number for the commits pushed directly.

Time to main is described in its own part above. The table shows the median by default, so the figure is the median time to main.

Notes

Below the table the list Notes, open by default, has one line for each kind of note that occurs, with the projects it applies to and their counts. The numbers are fixed, so they mean the same everywhere.

  • (1) Mostly estimated: commits timed by author date. It applies to the Mainline Interval.
  • (2) Commits timed by author date. It applies to the Mainline Interval.
  • (3) A lower bound: a first commit time of a pull request was cut. It applies to PR Lead Time and Bot PR Lead Time.
  • (4) Pull requests timed by their created time. It applies to PR Lead Time and Bot PR Lead Time.
  • (6) Pushes left out: their commits are not known (incomplete data), not counted as 0. It applies to Mainline Lead Time.
  • (7) History still filling: merge and squash branch commits from before this version are missing. It applies to Mainline Lead Time.
  • (8) Files still being read: commits whose changed files forsgren has not read yet are left out. It applies to Time to deploy per commit and Commits per deployment of a service with paths.

Week page

The Week page shows, for each project and under it for each label, service or rule, the deployments, the failed deployments and the fail rate of the last {{.Window}} days, per weekday from Monday to Sunday. It counts the period the footer names as Report Start and Report End, and every weekday of that window, not one calendar week, so a weekday appears as often as the window holds it. Days are in UTC.

Total has the columns Deployments, Failed and Fail rate, and under each weekday the columns are Deploys, Failed and Fail rate. In Total, Deployments is the count of the row's deployments of the window. Under each weekday, Deploys is the share of the row's deployments that fall on that weekday, written as the shares on the TBD page are, and the seven shares of a column add up to 100% before rounding. Under each weekday the header Deploys stands for deployments and saves width; a screen reader hears the full word. With week_commits: true, Commits comes first in Total and under each weekday: in Total the count of the row's commits, and under each weekday the share of them that fall on that weekday. A commit is counted at its author date and a deployment at its time. The commits are the ones counted as Commits on the TBD page: the first-parent commits that the pushes to the main branch brought in the window, each once. The deployments are the successful ones of the window. A weekday without any reads 0%, and a 0 is zero. A row with no commits, or no deployments, reads 0 in Total and a dash in its weekday cells, as there is nothing to divide.

Failed and Fail rate count the failed deployments of the window: those with the state failure. A deployment that finished in another way is not failed. Under each weekday, Failed is the share of the row's failed deployments that fall on that weekday, and Fail rate is the failed deployments of that weekday out of its successful plus failed deployments. Deployments that finished in another way are left out of Fail rate, in the failed deployments and in the total, as in the change fail rate. The successful deployments alone are counted in the Deployments column, so that column is not the denominator. In Total, Failed is a count and Fail rate is the failed deployments out of the successful plus failed deployments of the window. A dash means there is nothing to divide: a row with no failed deployments has a dash in its Failed cells, and a weekday without successful or failed deployments has a dash in its Fail rate cell. In each row the weekday with the highest Failed and the weekday with the highest Fail rate are shown in red, every tied weekday, and none when the row has no failed deployments; a screen reader hears the word highest in these cells.

A service or rule with paths in the configuration counts its own commits: the commits of its deployments that change one of its paths, each once, as Deployed commits on the TBD page. A service or rule without paths cannot tell which commits belong to it. Its Total Commits and its Commits cells read –: the en dash stands for commits not attributed to this service or rule, which are counted at repository level on the project's row. It is not zero commits, and it does not mean deployment data is missing; its Deployments cells are the percentages of the deployments of the service or rule itself.

With two projects or more, the first row, Total, right under the column headers, adds up the rows of every project: its counts are the sums of all projects, per weekday and in Total, its shares are the shares of those sums, and its Fail rate is the failed deployments out of the successful plus failed deployments of all projects. A label, service or rule row is not added again, as the row of its project already counts it. Its highest weekdays are shown in red as in every row. The Total row is shaded, so it stands apart from the project rows. With one project there is no Total row, as it would only repeat the project's row.

The Commits columns are off by default: set week_commits: true to show them under Total and under each weekday.

The Week page is in the menu by default. Set week_page: false to take it out of the menu; it is still generated, can be opened at week.html, and search engines are asked to skip it.

Data page

The Data page shows, for each project and under it for each label, service or rule, the first date forsgren stored data of each kind: Issues, Commits, Pushes, Deployments and Merged pull requests. Each date is a day in UTC. A date marked ▲ is after Report Start: the window is only partly covered, and the metrics of that kind count from that date on. A dash means nothing of that kind is stored. A date marked … is only where the read of a repository's pushes stopped at its page limit: earlier data is not yet read, so it does not mean the repository started on that date. It marks Pushes, and Commits when the pushed commits give its date, and such a date is red; the … says it without the colour. A date marked ⇤ is as far back as GitHub keeps the activity of a repository: its list ends there without the creation of the branch, so GitHub keeps no older activity. It is not a complete history, and not where the repository started. Each explanation above the table appears only when a date on the page carries its marker.

Commits is the oldest author date over the deployed commits and the pushed commits; a service or rule row has its own deployed commits only. A service or rule row reads Per repository for Issues, Pushes and Merged pull requests, as these are read per repository, not per service or rule. A project's date is the earliest over its repositories. The first stored date is when forsgren started collecting that data, not when the repository started. Under the table, Which pages use which data names the pages each kind feeds.

The Data page is linked in the footer, next to Settings, Legend and Dictionary, by default. Set data_page: unlisted to take the link away; the page is still generated, can be opened at data.html, and search engines are asked to skip it. Set data_page: false to leave it out.

Settings page

The Default column of the settings page is the value used when forsgren.config.yml does not set the key. A current value followed by (not set) means the file does not set the key, so the default applies; a current value without it is set in the file, even to the default's value.

auto_update_level is the largest step of a forsgren update that merges itself; a larger one is left for you. By what a release asks of an installation, a patch release is fixes only, with nothing new for the installation to do or see; a minor release is new behaviour, or anything that asks the installation for something (a permission, a secret, a file, a config key); a major release is one where an installation that does nothing would stop working.

  • patch: merges patch updates only
  • minor: merges minor and patch updates
  • major: merges major, minor and patch updates. It does not mean a major release is safe: nothing checks a release against this installation's own data.
